Is a Master's Degree or MBA Worth It? A Return-on-Investment Framework

A step-by-step ROI framework for a master's or MBA: total cost with opportunity cost, realistic salary uplift, payback period, risk and non-financial value.

Paying for school6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

A master's degree or MBA can pay off handsomely, or leave you with debt and no raise. The difference usually comes down to numbers you can estimate before you apply. This guide gives you a five-step framework: total cost, realistic salary uplift, payback period, risk and non-financial value, with a worked example you can adapt to your own situation.

Step 1: Add up the full cost, including what you give up

Most people compare tuition and stop there. The full cost has three parts:

  • Direct costs. Tuition, mandatory fees, books, technology and any travel or residency sessions. Use the program's official fee page for the year you'd enrol, and assume fees may rise in later years.
  • Extra living costs. Only count what the degree adds: relocation, a higher-cost city, or an extra year of rent you wouldn't otherwise pay. Living costs you'd have anyway don't belong in the calculation.
  • Opportunity cost. The after-tax income you give up by studying instead of working, plus lost retirement contributions and any promotion you might have earned in the meantime. For full-time programs this is often the biggest number on the page.

If you'll borrow, add the total interest you expect to pay over the life of the loan. The loan calculator can estimate it.

Step 2: Estimate a realistic salary uplift

The uplift is not "what master's graduates earn". It's the difference between what you're likely to earn with the degree and what you're likely to earn without it over the same years. Your no-degree path includes raises and promotions too.

Where to find credible data

  • Official labour-market data. In the US, the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Outlook Handbook shows pay and typical entry education by occupation, and the College Scorecard reports earnings by field of study for many programs, including some graduate ones. In Canada, Job Bank publishes wages by occupation and region. In Australia, the QILT surveys report graduate outcomes, including for postgraduate coursework. The UK government and HESA publish graduate outcomes data as well.
  • Program outcome reports. Many programs, especially MBAs, publish employment reports. Check the median (not just average) salary, the percentage of graduates who reported, the time frame (for example, three months after graduation) and how much work experience students had before enrolling.
  • Job postings and people in the role. Look at postings for the job you want. Do they require the degree, prefer it, or not mention it? Informational conversations with people already in the role can tell you whether the credential actually moved their pay.

Watch out: Graduate salary figures can overstate what the degree itself causes. People who enrol in competitive programs often had strong earning prospects already. Be conservative and estimate the uplift for someone with your background, not the program's best-case graduate.

Step 3: Calculate the payback period

The payback period is the number of years it takes for the extra after-tax income to cover the total cost. It's a simplified measure, but it makes trade-offs visible fast.

Payback period = total cost ÷ annual after-tax uplift

A hypothetical worked example

Say you currently earn 50,000 a year. You're considering a one-year, full-time master's with 30,000 in tuition and fees, plus 2,000 in extra costs. For simplicity, assume a flat 30 percent tax rate. These numbers are illustrative only, in any currency.

  1. Opportunity cost. You give up a year's salary: 50,000 × 0.70 = 35,000 after tax.
  2. Total cost. 30,000 + 2,000 + 35,000 = 67,000.
  3. Uplift. Your research suggests you'd earn 62,000 with the degree versus 50,000 on your current path, a 12,000 gain. After tax: 12,000 × 0.70 = 8,400 a year.
  4. Payback. 67,000 ÷ 8,400 ≈ 8 years.

Now change one assumption at a time:

ScenarioTotal costAfter-tax upliftPayback
Full-time, as above67,0008,400About 8 years
Part-time while working32,0008,400About 3.8 years
Part-time, employer pays 10,00022,0008,400About 2.6 years
Full-time, uplift only 6,000 before tax67,0004,200About 16 years

Two lessons stand out. Opportunity cost dominates full-time study, and the payback period is very sensitive to the uplift estimate, which is the number most people get wrong. Note that part-time study delays the uplift until you finish, and this model ignores raises, interest and the time value of money. To run your own numbers, use the ROI calculator.

Step 4: Price in the risk

A spreadsheet assumes everything goes to plan. Ask what happens if it doesn't:

  • Completion risk: could work, family or health interrupt your studies? Part-time and online formats can lower the stakes.
  • Labour-market risk: is demand for your target role stable, or tied to one industry or region?
  • Debt risk: could you make loan payments if your salary didn't rise for several years?
  • Quality risk: is the program properly accredited? See our guide on checking accreditation.

A useful rule of thumb: if your plan only works under the optimistic uplift, it's a risky plan. Look for a version, such as part-time study or employer funding, that still makes sense under the conservative estimate.

Step 5: Weigh the value that doesn't fit in a spreadsheet

Some reasons to pursue a graduate degree are real but hard to price:

  • Access. Some regulated professions and academic or research careers require a graduate degree. If that's your goal, the question isn't whether to get the degree but which program offers the best value. Check requirements with the relevant regulator or licensing body.
  • Career change. A structured program can open doors when switching fields, through internships, projects and an alumni network.
  • Interest and fulfilment. Deep study of a subject you care about has value of its own. Just be honest with yourself about which reason is driving the decision.
  • Mobility. In some countries, graduate study can affect post-study work options. Rules change often, so check the official immigration authority. See our study abroad hub.

Ways to cut the cost: sponsorship, part-time and online

Employer sponsorship. Many employers offer tuition assistance or full sponsorship. Ask HR before you apply, and read the agreement carefully: sponsorships often require you to stay for a set period or repay the money if you leave.

Part-time and online programs. Keeping your salary removes most of the opportunity cost. Compare the accreditation, curriculum and outcomes against the full-time version rather than assuming they're equal.

Government support. Postgraduate funding rules vary widely. England offers a Postgraduate Master's Loan through GOV.UK. In the US, federal borrowing rules for graduate students changed under 2025 legislation, so check current limits on studentaid.gov. Elsewhere, check your national student-aid site and your university's funding page.

Scholarships and assistantships. Graduate scholarships, teaching or research assistantships, and fee waivers can cut costs sharply. Start with our scholarships page.

Frequently asked questions

What's a good payback period for a master's degree?

There's no universal benchmark. Shorter is safer, and you should compare the payback period with how long you expect to stay in the field. A plan that takes most of your remaining career to pay back deserves a hard second look.

Is an MBA different from other master's degrees?

The framework is the same, but MBA outcomes often depend heavily on prior work experience and the program's employer network. Read employment reports closely and compare medians for people with backgrounds like yours.

Should I work for a few years before a master's?

Often, yes. Work experience clarifies your goals, can strengthen your application and may make you eligible for employer sponsorship. Exceptions include fields where the degree is required to enter the profession.
