Coding bootcamps: what to check before you pay

Before you pay for a coding bootcamp, learn to read job-placement claims, judge curriculum and instructors, understand ISAs, and test your fit for free.

Programs6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

A coding bootcamp can be a fast route into tech or an expensive lesson. Before you sign, check how the provider counts its outcomes, what you'll actually learn, who will teach you, and exactly what the payment contract commits you to. This guide covers each check and shows you how to test your fit for free first.

Test your fit for free first

The cheapest way to reduce bootcamp risk is to find out whether you enjoy programming before you pay anyone. Several respected, free resources cover the same foundations that bootcamps teach in their first weeks:

  • freeCodeCamp: a nonprofit with a free, self-paced curriculum in web development and related topics, plus free certifications.
  • The Odin Project: a free, open-source curriculum that takes you through full-stack web development by building projects.
  • CS50: Harvard University's introduction to computer science, with lectures and problem sets available free online.

Set yourself a trial: a fixed number of hours a week for four to six weeks, on a schedule that resembles the one the bootcamp would demand. If you can't sustain it, or you dread the sessions, an intensive program will be harder still. If you love it, you'll arrive at a bootcamp better prepared, and you may decide you can keep learning without one.

How to read job-placement claims

A headline such as "90% of graduates hired" can be technically true and still misleading. The number depends entirely on who is counted and what counts as a job. Ask for the full outcomes report, not the marketing summary, and look for these details.

What to checkWhy it matters
Who is in the denominatorA rate based only on "job-seeking graduates" leaves out everyone who dropped out or stopped searching.
What counts as "employed in field"Some reports include part-time work, short contracts, freelance gigs, internships, teaching-assistant jobs at the bootcamp itself, or roles that don't involve coding.
The time window"Hired within 180 days" and "hired within a year" are very different claims.
Response rateIf many graduates didn't report back, the rate reflects only those who did, who may be the most successful.
Salary figuresCheck whether it's a median or an average, how many people reported, and whether it includes people who already worked in tech.
Which cohorts and who checkedRecent cohorts tell you more than old ones, and independently reviewed figures are more trustworthy than self-reported ones.

Here's how the gap opens up, using hypothetical numbers. Say 100 people start a cohort, 80 graduate, the school classifies 60 of those as job-seeking, and 48 find some kind of paid role within six months. The school can advertise an 80% placement rate (48 of 60), yet only 48% of the people who started found any job, and fewer may hold full-time developer roles.

Curriculum depth

Ask for the full syllabus and a sample project, and check whether the program covers fundamentals as well as the current popular framework. Strong programs typically include:

  • Core programming concepts, problem solving and basic data structures
  • Version control with Git and collaborative workflows
  • Databases, APIs and how front end and back end connect
  • Testing, debugging and deploying a working application
  • Several substantial projects you build and can explain yourself, not just follow-along exercises

Ask how many hours are live instruction versus self-paced material, and when the curriculum was last updated. Check whether the languages taught match the job postings in your area.

Instructors and support

Ask for the student-to-instructor ratio and the student-to-mentor ratio separately, because teaching assistants and mentors are sometimes recent graduates of the same program. Find out who the lead instructors are, what professional experience they have, how often your code will be reviewed, and how quickly you can get help when you're stuck.

Ask what happens if you fall behind. Can you repeat a module, and does that cost extra? Is career support included, and does it continue after you graduate?

Financing: read every line

Bootcamps offer several ways to pay: upfront tuition, instalments, loans arranged through outside lenders, and two models that deserve particular care.

  • Income share agreements (ISAs): you pay nothing or little upfront, then pay a percentage of your income for a set period once you earn above a minimum threshold, usually up to a maximum total.
  • Deferred tuition: you pay after the program, often in fixed monthly amounts once you start a job or after a set date. These plans are frequently loans under another name.

Before you sign either, get the full contract in advance and find these terms:

  • The percentage or payment amount, and the income threshold that triggers payments
  • The maximum you could repay in total, compared with the upfront price
  • How long payments last, and the longest period the contract can run
  • What counts as income: any job, or only work in tech
  • What happens if you withdraw early, lose your job or move abroad
  • Interest, fees, late-payment penalties and credit reporting
  • Whether the contract can be sold to another company, and how disputes are handled

Watch out: Regulators in some places have treated ISAs and deferred-tuition plans as loans covered by lending law, and the rules differ by country, state and province. Read the contract in full and check your local consumer-protection authority's guidance before you sign. If anything is unclear, ask for the answer in writing.

Guarantees, refunds and registration

Some bootcamps offer a "job guarantee" or tuition refund if you don't find work. Read the conditions: they can require you to apply to a set number of jobs every week, accept any relevant offer, or be willing to relocate. Missing one condition can void the guarantee.

In some US states and Canadian provinces, providers that charge for career training must be licensed or registered with an education regulator. Registration can bring refund rules and a formal complaints process, so ask whether the bootcamp is registered where you live and check the regulator's list yourself.

Your final pre-payment checklist

  • You've completed a free trial period and still want to do this.
  • You've read the full outcomes report and understand its definitions.
  • The syllabus matches job postings in your area.
  • You know the instructor and mentor ratios and who teaches.
  • You have the payment contract and the refund policy, and you've read both.
  • You know whether the provider is registered with a regulator where you live.
  • You've compared it with alternatives, such as a college certificate or diploma.

Frequently asked questions

Do employers hire bootcamp graduates?

Some do, but employers mostly judge what you can show: projects, code samples and how you perform in technical interviews. Check job postings in your area to see whether they ask for a degree, experience or a portfolio.

Is a bootcamp better than a computer science degree?

They serve different goals. A degree takes longer and covers more theory, and some employers and roles require one. A bootcamp is shorter and more applied. Which is better depends on the jobs you're targeting and your existing education.

Can I learn to code without a bootcamp?

Yes. Many working developers are largely self-taught using free resources. What a good bootcamp adds is structure, feedback, deadlines and career support, which some people need and others don't.

Can I get government funding for a bootcamp?

Sometimes. Bootcamps usually aren't eligible for the student aid that covers colleges and universities, but some public employment or workforce programs fund approved short training. Ask your local employment service which providers qualify.
