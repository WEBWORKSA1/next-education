How to verify a school's accreditation and spot diploma mills

Check whether a college or university is recognised in the US, Canada, UK, India or Australia using official registers, and spot the red flags of diploma mills.

Planning6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

"Accredited" is one of the most abused words in education marketing. This guide shows you how to check a school's status in the official registers of the US, Canada, the UK, India and Australia, explains what each check does and doesn't prove, and gives you a checklist for spotting diploma mills.

What accreditation actually tells you

Accreditation or official recognition means a government agency or a recognised accrediting body has reviewed an institution, or a specific program, against quality standards and keeps monitoring it. It affects whether your credits transfer, whether employers and regulators accept your credential, and in some countries whether you can get government student aid.

The question to ask is never just "Is it accredited?" but "Accredited by whom?" Diploma mills often claim approval from accreditation mills: organisations with official-sounding names that no government recognises. Always verify through the official source for that country, never through a link or certificate the school sends you.

Keep two layers in mind. Institutional recognition covers the whole school. Program or professional accreditation covers a single program, and in regulated fields such as nursing, medicine, engineering or law it can matter as much as the institution's status.

United States

The US Department of Education doesn't accredit schools itself. It recognises accrediting agencies, and those agencies accredit institutions and programs. The Council for Higher Education Accreditation (CHEA), a non-governmental organisation, also recognises accreditors.

  • Search the Department's Database of Accredited Postsecondary Institutions and Programs (DAPIP) for the school and note which agency accredits it.
  • Check that agency against the Department's or CHEA's list of recognised accreditors. If it appears on neither, treat the accreditation claim as worthless.
  • For licensed careers, confirm the program holds the programmatic accreditation your state licensing board requires.

In general, federal student aid can only be used at institutions accredited by a Department-recognised agency. You'll still see accreditors described as "regional" or "national." What matters more is whether the accreditor is recognised and whether schools you might later transfer to accept its credits.

Canada

Canada has no national accreditation system. Education is a provincial and territorial responsibility, so recognition comes from the province or territory. Public universities and colleges are generally established or authorised under provincial legislation, and private career colleges are typically registered with a provincial regulator.

  • Start with the Canadian Information Centre for International Credentials (CICIC), whose directory of educational institutions points to provincial and territorial sources.
  • Check the relevant ministry's list of authorised institutions or registered private career colleges.
  • If you're an international student, the school must be a designated learning institution (DLI) on Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada's (IRCC) list for you to get a study permit.

A DLI number means a school is approved to host international students. It isn't a quality ranking, and not every program at a DLI leads to a post-graduation work permit, so check IRCC's eligibility rules for your specific program.

United Kingdom

In the UK, only certain institutions can award degrees. These are known as recognised bodies. Other providers teach courses that lead to a degree awarded by a recognised body; in some parts of the UK these are called listed bodies.

  • In England, search the Office for Students (OfS) Register of higher education providers.
  • Use GOV.UK's "Check if your university or college can award a degree" guidance, which links to the official lists for England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  • Find out who actually awards your degree. It may be a partner university rather than the college where you study, so check the awarding body's status too.

Employers who need to confirm a UK degree can use Hedd (Higher Education Degree Datacheck), the UK's official degree verification service.

India

India has several bodies, each with a different job. Check every one that applies to your course.

  • University Grants Commission (UGC). Publishes lists of recognised universities and regularly issues public warnings naming self-styled "fake universities" that have no right to award degrees. UGC also regulates open, distance and online programs, so check that an online degree is offered by an institution UGC has entitled to run it.
  • All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE). Approves technical programs such as engineering, technology and management at institutions that require its approval. Check the approval for your specific course and academic year.
  • National Assessment and Accreditation Council (NAAC). Assesses and grades higher education institutions on quality. A NAAC grade is useful for comparison, but it isn't the same thing as legal recognition.

Professional fields have their own statutory councils, such as the National Medical Commission for medicine and the Indian Nursing Council for nursing. Confirm a program is recognised by the relevant council before you apply.

Australia

Australia regulates higher education and vocational education and training (VET) separately.

  • Higher education: every provider must be registered with the Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA). Search TEQSA's National Register for the provider and its registration category.
  • VET: most registered training organisations are regulated by the Australian Skills Quality Authority (ASQA). Search training.gov.au to confirm the provider is registered and allowed to deliver the specific qualification.
  • International students: both the provider and the course must also be listed on CRICOS, the Commonwealth register of institutions and courses for overseas students.

Red flags: how to spot a diploma mill

  • The accrediting body isn't on the official government or recognised list for that country.
  • You can earn a degree mainly or entirely for "life experience," with little or no assessed coursework.
  • The degree has a flat price, or you can pay extra to choose grades, honours or a graduation date.
  • Completion is promised in days or weeks for a qualification that normally takes years.
  • There are no real admission requirements, or acceptance is instant.
  • The name closely imitates a well-known university.
  • There's no verifiable physical address, and the faculty can't be found anywhere else online.
  • Sales tactics are aggressive: "today only" discounts, repeated calls, pressure to pay now.
  • The school's own website is the only place its accreditation is mentioned.

Watch out: A web address that looks academic isn't proof of legitimacy. Only the official register for the country counts, and you should open that register yourself rather than clicking a link the school provides.

A five-minute verification routine

  1. Get the exact legal name. Use the name on the school's official documents, plus its city and country, not a brand or campus name.
  2. Search the official register yourself. Type the regulator's address into your browser instead of following links from the school or a recruiter.
  3. Check who vouches for the school. Confirm that the accreditor or regulator named is itself recognised.
  4. Check the program, not just the school. For regulated careers, confirm program-level approval and licensing eligibility in the place you plan to work.
  5. Keep a record. Save a dated screenshot of the register entry in case anything changes after you enrol.

Frequently asked questions

Does accreditation guarantee my credits will transfer?

No. Accreditation makes transfer possible, but each receiving institution decides which credits it accepts. If you expect to transfer, ask the school you'd move to, in writing, before you enrol.

Is an online degree from a recognised school legitimate?

Yes, as long as the institution and the specific program are properly recognised. Many recognised universities award the same degree to online and on-campus students. Check that the online program itself is covered, especially in countries such as India where online programs need separate entitlement.

Is every unaccredited provider a scam?

Not necessarily. Some legitimate providers offer short, non-credit courses that don't seek accreditation. But their certificates usually won't count toward licences, credit transfer or government student aid, so know what you're buying.

What should I do if I've already paid a diploma mill?

Stop any further payments, keep every receipt and message, and report the provider to your national, state or provincial consumer-protection authority or education regulator. Don't list the credential on job applications, because presenting a fake degree as genuine can have serious consequences.
