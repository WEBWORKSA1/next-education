How to judge an online degree program: a 12-point checklist

Use this 12-point checklist to judge an online degree before you enrol: accreditation, format, faculty, support, total cost, transfer credit and outcomes.

Programs6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

An online degree can be as rigorous and respected as a campus degree, or an expensive dead end. These 12 checks help you tell the difference before you enrol, with the questions to put to admissions and the places to verify their answers independently.

First, pin down what "online" means

Online programs come in several formats. Some are fully online. Hybrid programs mix online courses with regular campus sessions. Low-residency programs, common at graduate level, are mostly online with short, intensive periods on campus.

Ask exactly which parts must be done in person, including labs, clinical or teaching placements, and exams, and where they happen. Travel adds cost and time, and a placement you can't arrange locally can stall your progress.

Legitimacy checks

1. Recognised accreditation

Confirm the institution is recognised in the official register for its country, and that the online program is covered. Don't rely on logos on the school's website. Our accreditation guide lists the registers for the US, Canada, the UK, India and Australia. In the US, also ask whether the school is authorised to enrol students who live in your state.

2. The same degree as on campus

Ask whether the diploma and transcript are identical to those given to campus students, and whether admission standards and curriculum match. Some universities run online programs with an outside company handling marketing or technology. That can be fine, but the university itself should award the degree and control the teaching and assessment. Ask who does what.

3. Recognition where you'll work

For licensed careers such as nursing, teaching, counselling or social work, the program has to meet the licensing rules where you plan to practise, which may not be where the school is based. Get the answer in writing. If you're studying with a school in another country, check whether employers and regulators at home will recognise the degree.

How you'll learn

4. Synchronous or asynchronous

Synchronous courses meet live at set times. Asynchronous courses let you work through material on your own schedule within weekly deadlines. Live sessions give structure and real-time discussion but can clash with work, caregiving or your time zone. Asynchronous study is flexible but demands steady self-discipline. Ask for a typical weekly schedule and the expected hours per course.

5. Who teaches

Ask whether online courses are designed and taught by the same faculty as campus courses, and how many are taught by full-time versus part-time instructors. Find out who grades your work and how quickly instructors are expected to respond to questions.

6. Class size and interaction

Ask how many students are in a typical online section and how you'll interact with instructors and classmates: discussion boards, live seminars, group projects or one-on-one feedback. If possible, preview a sample course or attend an online information session that shows the learning platform.

Support and technology

7. Student support built for online learners

Online students need the same services as campus students, delivered remotely. Check for academic advising, tutoring, writing help, library databases, disability accommodations, counselling and career services. Ask whether tech support is available during the hours you'll actually study.

8. Proctoring and technology requirements

Many online programs use remote proctoring for exams, which can involve webcam monitoring, ID checks, a scan of your room and software installed on your computer. Ask what's required, whether there are extra fees, what happens if your connection drops mid-exam, and whether an in-person test centre is an option. Confirm the minimum computer, internet and software requirements too.

Money

9. The total cost, including fees

Online tuition isn't always cheaper than on-campus tuition, and the advertised per-credit price rarely tells the whole story. Build a full estimate:

  • Tuition per credit or per term, multiplied by the credits needed to graduate
  • Technology, online learning, distance or program fees
  • Proctoring fees and required software
  • Textbooks, digital access codes and materials
  • Travel and accommodation for any residencies or placements
  • Application and graduation fees

Ask whether tuition is locked for the length of the program or can rise each year, and request the whole-program estimate in writing. The budget planner and loan calculator help you test whether it fits.

10. Financial aid, refunds and pauses

Ask whether the program qualifies for government student aid where you live, whether your employer offers tuition support, and what the refund schedule is if you withdraw. Find out what happens if you need to pause for a term. Rules for distance learners can differ from campus rules, so confirm with your official student aid body. Our financial aid basics guide covers the main options.

Your path forward

11. Transfer credit and prior learning

Ask how many credits the program will accept from previous study, and whether it offers prior learning assessment for work experience or recognised certifications. Request an unofficial credit evaluation before you enrol. Then ask the reverse question: if you leave, will other institutions accept these credits?

12. Outcomes data

Ask for completion rates, retention, licensing exam pass rates where relevant, and employment outcomes, ideally for online students specifically, with a clear explanation of how each figure was calculated. Cross-check with official sources where they exist: College Scorecard in the US, Discover Uni in the UK and QILT's ComparED in Australia. A school that can't or won't share outcomes is telling you something.

Recruiter pressure red flags

Legitimate programs employ admissions staff, but good ones answer questions in writing and give you time to decide. Be wary if you notice any of the following.

  • Repeated calls or messages pushing you to enrol before you've seen the full cost.
  • Deadlines or discounts that appear suddenly and expire within days.
  • Requests for payment or bank details on a first call.
  • Promises of guaranteed jobs, salaries or admission regardless of your background.
  • Vague answers about accreditation, licensing eligibility or who awards the degree.
  • Playing down the workload, such as suggesting a full degree takes only a few hours a week.
  • Reluctance to put any answer in writing.

Quick check: Email your key questions to admissions and keep the replies. A school confident in its answers won't mind putting them on record.

Frequently asked questions

Do employers respect online degrees?

Most employers focus on the institution, its recognition and your skills rather than the delivery format. Many recognised universities award the same degree to online and campus students; ask whether yours mentions the delivery format on the diploma or transcript. Check job postings in your field if you're unsure.

Is asynchronous study easier?

It's more flexible, not easier. Without set class times you have to plan your own week and keep up with deadlines. If you struggle to study without structure, a program with some live sessions may suit you better.

Can I get financial aid for an online degree?

Often, if the institution and program are eligible under your country's rules. In the US, eligible online programs at participating schools can qualify for federal student aid. Elsewhere, check with your national, state or provincial student aid body, since rules for distance learning can differ.

How many hours a week should I expect?

It depends on your course load and the program's credit system. Ask the program for its expected weekly hours per course and multiply by the number of courses you plan to take at once.
