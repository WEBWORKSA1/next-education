Grants, Loans and Work-Study: Financial Aid Basics

Understand need-based vs merit aid, grants vs loans vs work-study, how loan interest accrues, and where to apply in the US, Canada, UK, India and Australia.

Paying for school6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

Financial aid comes in three main forms: money you don't repay, money you borrow, and money you earn while you study. This guide explains how each one works, how loan interest quietly grows, and where to apply in the US, Canada, the UK, India and Australia, so you can build an aid package that keeps your debt as low as possible.

Need-based vs merit-based aid

Aid is awarded on two broad grounds, and many students receive both.

  • Need-based aid depends on your financial situation. Governments and schools assess your income (and often your family's), household size and sometimes assets, then estimate what you can afford. The gap between that figure and the cost of attendance is your "need".
  • Merit-based aid rewards achievement: grades, test results, talent, leadership or service. It's mostly offered by schools and private funders rather than governments.

The practical lesson: apply for need-based aid even if you think you won't qualify. Formulas consider more than income, and many schools and scholarships require a government aid application before they will consider you for their own funds.

How loan interest works

Interest is the price of borrowing. The key question with any loan is when interest starts to build, and who pays it while you're studying.

Subsidized vs unsubsidized

With a subsidized loan, someone else, usually the government, pays the interest during certain periods, such as while you're enrolled. Your balance stays the same until repayment begins. With an unsubsidized loan, interest accrues from the day the money is paid out, including while you study. In the US, these terms are formal loan names. In other countries the structure differs, but the same question applies: is interest building while I'm in school?

Accrual and capitalization

Here's a hypothetical example with round numbers. Say you borrow 10,000 on an unsubsidized loan at an illustrative 6 percent simple annual rate and make no payments during four years of study. Interest grows by 600 a year (10,000 × 0.06), so you would owe 2,400 in interest by graduation, for a total of 12,400.

Depending on the loan and the country, unpaid interest may then be capitalized, meaning it's added to your principal. From that point, you pay interest on the interest too. Paying even small amounts of interest while you study prevents this. To test your own numbers, try the loan calculator.

Quick check: Rates, borrowing limits and repayment rules change, often yearly. Always confirm current figures on the official government site listed below rather than relying on a blog post or a figure you heard from a friend.

Where to apply: country notes

United States

Start with the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) at studentaid.gov. It's free to file and is the gateway to federal grants such as the Pell Grant, Federal Work-Study and federal Direct Loans. Many states and colleges use FAFSA data for their own aid, and some private colleges also require the CSS Profile. Direct Subsidized Loans are for undergraduates with financial need; Direct Unsubsidized Loans accrue interest from disbursement. Federal borrowing rules for graduate students changed under 2025 legislation, so check current limits on studentaid.gov before planning graduate study.

Canada

The federal Canada Student Financial Assistance Program provides Canada Student Grants and Loans, and you normally apply through your province or territory's student aid program, such as OSAP in Ontario. Quebec, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut run their own programs. The federal government eliminated interest on the federal portion of Canada Student Loans in 2023; provincial portions follow provincial rules, so check both.

United Kingdom

Student finance depends on where you live: Student Finance England (via GOV.UK), Student Awards Agency Scotland, Student Finance Wales or Student Finance NI. The main products are a Tuition Fee Loan, paid directly to your university, and a Maintenance Loan for living costs. Repayments are income-contingent: you repay only when you earn above a threshold, and the rules depend on your repayment plan. Universities also offer bursaries, so check your university's website.

India

The National Scholarship Portal (scholarships.gov.in) brings central government and many state scholarship schemes into one application. For education loans, the government's PM-Vidyalaxmi portal lets you compare and apply to participating banks. Before borrowing, ask each lender about collateral, the repayment moratorium, and whether interest accrues during it. Many states also run their own scholarship portals.

Australia

The government's HELP loans, explained on StudyAssist, cover tuition: HECS-HELP for Commonwealth supported places, and FEE-HELP for many full-fee places. You repay through the tax system once your income passes a threshold, and balances are indexed rather than charged commercial interest. For living costs, check student payments through Services Australia.

Build your aid package in the right order

  1. Apply for government aid early. File your national or provincial aid application as soon as it opens; some aid is limited and handed out until funds run out.
  2. Maximise gift aid. Accept every grant, bursary and scholarship first. See the scholarships page and our weekly scholarship system.
  3. Add earnings. Work-study, campus jobs and part-time work reduce what you need to borrow.
  4. Borrow government loans before private ones. Government loans usually offer income-linked repayment, hardship relief or protections that private lenders may not.
  5. Treat private loans as the last resort. Compare the total cost of borrowing, not just the monthly payment.

Read your aid offer carefully

Aid offers don't always separate gift aid from loans clearly, and some call everything an "award". Before you accept, sort every line into gift aid, earned aid or borrowed money. Then subtract only the gift aid from the full cost of attendance, including housing, food, books and transport. That's your real net price.

  • Is each grant or scholarship renewable, and on what conditions?
  • Which loans accrue interest while you study?
  • What happens to your aid if you drop to part-time?
  • Will outside scholarships reduce your need-based aid?
  • Does your monthly budget actually balance? Test it with the budget planner.

If your circumstances have changed since you applied, such as a job loss or a family emergency, contact the financial-aid office. Many schools can review your aid when circumstances change.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to repay a grant?

Generally no, but grants often come with conditions. If you withdraw, drop below the required course load or don't make satisfactory progress, you may have to repay part of it.

Should I accept every loan I'm offered?

No. You can usually accept less than the full amount. Borrow what your budget shows you need, after gift aid and earnings, not the maximum available.

Can adult and part-time learners get financial aid?

Often, yes. Many government programs support part-time and mature students, although eligibility rules differ. Check your country's official student aid site and ask your school's aid office.

Is financial aid available for international students?

Government loans and grants are usually limited to citizens and residents. International students should look at institutional scholarships, home-country government schemes and reputable external funders.
