Evidence-based study techniques that actually work

Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving and other study methods backed by cognitive science, what to drop, and a sample weekly plan you can copy today.

Learning6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

Most students study the way they were never taught not to: rereading notes and highlighting textbooks. Decades of cognitive science point to better methods. This guide explains the techniques with the strongest evidence, how to use each one, and a weekly plan that puts them together.

What the research says

The most cited summary is a 2013 review by John Dunlosky and colleagues in Psychological Science in the Public Interest. The authors looked at ten common study techniques and rated how useful each was, based on how well the evidence held up across different learners, materials and tests.

Rating in the reviewTechniques
High utilityPractice testing, distributed (spaced) practice
Moderate utilityElaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice
Low utilitySummarisation, highlighting and underlining, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for learning text, rereading

"Low utility" does not mean useless. It means the benefits were limited, inconsistent or not well enough studied to recommend widely. The Learning Scientists, a group of cognitive psychologists, turned this research into six practical strategies: spaced practice, retrieval practice, elaboration, interleaving, concrete examples and dual coding.

Retrieval practice: test yourself

Pulling information out of memory strengthens it more than putting it in again. In a well-known 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke found that students who practised recalling a passage remembered more of it days later than students who spent the same time restudying, even though restudying looked better on a test taken minutes afterwards.

  • Close your notes and write down everything you remember, then check what you missed.
  • Answer practice questions or past papers before you feel ready.
  • Make flashcards with a question on one side, and say the answer before you flip.
  • Explain a topic aloud as if teaching it, then check for gaps.

Quick check: Retrieval feels harder than rereading, and that effort is part of why it works. If you cannot explain a topic without your notes open, you do not know it well enough yet.

Spaced practice: spread it out

The same total study time works better when it is spread over days and weeks than when it is crammed into one session. This spacing effect is one of the most replicated findings in memory research, and a 2006 meta-analysis by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues pulled together hundreds of experiments supporting it.

In practice, review new material within a day or two, again a few days later, then after a week or more. Spaced-repetition flashcard apps automate the scheduling. Without an app, keep a simple calendar of what to revisit and when. Cramming can get you through tomorrow's quiz, but it tends to fade fast.

Interleaving, elaboration, dual coding and worked examples

Interleaving

Instead of practising one type of problem in a block, mix related types in a single session. Interleaving forces you to decide which method fits each problem, which is exactly what exams ask. It suits maths, physics and any subject with problem types that look alike. It feels slower, but learning often improves.

Elaboration and self-explanation

Ask yourself how and why things work, and connect new ideas to what you already know. Why does this enzyme work faster at higher temperatures, and why does it stop working when it gets too hot? How is this case like the one from last week? Explaining each step of a solution to yourself builds understanding, not just recall.

Dual coding

Combine words with visuals: diagrams, timelines, flowcharts, labelled sketches. Representing an idea in two forms gives you more routes back to it. This is different from the popular idea of "learning styles"; matching teaching to a preferred style is not well supported by research, while combining words and pictures helps learners generally.

Worked examples

When a topic is new, studying a fully solved problem step by step is often more effective than struggling alone, because it reduces the mental load of figuring out where to start. Research in cognitive load theory, associated with John Sweller, supports this. As you improve, fade the support: cover the later steps and complete them yourself, then move to solving problems from scratch.

What works less well, and how to upgrade it

Rereading and highlighting are popular because they feel productive. The text becomes familiar, and familiarity is easy to mistake for knowing. That illusion fades the moment you face a blank exam paper.

  • Instead of rereading, try to recall first, then reread only the parts you got wrong.
  • Instead of highlighting, turn each key point into a question you can answer later.
  • Instead of copying notes neatly, rewrite them from memory and compare.
  • Instead of one long session the night before, split the same time across the week.

Sleep, focus and energy

Sleep plays a central role in consolidating memories, so an all-nighter trades tomorrow's retention for tonight's hours. Health bodies such as the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommend at least seven hours a night for adults and more for teenagers.

Focus matters as much as hours. Put your phone in another room, close unrelated tabs, and work in timed blocks with short breaks; the Pomodoro-style pattern of about 25 minutes on and 5 off is a common starting point. Our study timer runs these blocks for you. Short walks and regular exercise also help many people stay alert.

A sample weekly study plan

Here is a hypothetical week for a student taking two courses, using sessions of 45 to 60 minutes. Adjust the subjects and times to your own schedule.

DaySession focusTechniques used
MondayNew material from course AWorked examples, self-explanation, five-minute recall at the end
TuesdayNew material from course B; quick review of MondayDual coding (draw a diagram), flashcards for Monday's topic
WednesdayMixed practice problems from both weeks of course AInterleaving, retrieval
ThursdayCourse B review; questions on last week's topicsSpaced review, elaboration
FridayPractice quiz covering both coursesRetrieval, then reread only what you missed
SaturdayLight flashcard reviewSpaced repetition
SundayRest, plus a 15-minute plan for next weekNote which topics need another review

Start small. Swapping one rereading session a week for a self-test is a real improvement, and it is easier to keep up than a complete overhaul. The psychologist Robert Bjork calls the useful kind of struggle a "desirable difficulty": if studying feels a little hard, you are probably doing it right.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a study session be?

There is no single right length. Many students do well with focused blocks of 25 to 50 minutes and short breaks. What matters more is spreading sessions across the week and testing yourself within them.

Are flashcard apps worth using?

They can be, because they combine retrieval practice with spacing automatically. Write cards as questions, keep each card to one idea, and answer before you flip.

Does highlighting ever help?

It can help you spot key points on a first read, but it does little for memory on its own. Turn highlights into questions and test yourself on them.

Should I study according to my learning style?

Research has not found good evidence that matching study methods to a preferred learning style improves results. Use techniques like retrieval, spacing and dual coding, which help most learners.
