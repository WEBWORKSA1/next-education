How to budget for studying abroad

Build a realistic study abroad budget: every cost category, how proof of funds works, currency risk, part-time work limits and proven ways to spend less.

Study abroad6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

The tuition figure on a university website is only the start of what studying abroad costs. This guide walks you through every cost category, explains how proof of funds works, and shows you how to build a budget that survives exchange-rate swings and surprise bills.

Count the full cost, not just tuition

Students most often underestimate the costs that sit outside tuition. Build your budget from these categories, and get each number from an official or first-hand source rather than a forum post.

Some immigration costs are easy to miss. US F-1 and J-1 applicants generally pay a SEVIS fee as well as the visa application fee. UK applicants usually pay the Immigration Health Surcharge with their visa. Australia requires student visa holders to keep Overseas Student Health Cover. Check current amounts on the official sites.

Separate one-time and recurring costs

Your first months abroad cost far more than a typical month later on. Tuition deposits, visa fees, flights, rental deposits and setup purchases often land within a short window, and some are due before you leave home.

Split your budget into two parts: a one-time launch budget and a monthly running budget. The launch budget tells you how much cash you need up front. The running budget tells you whether your plan works for the whole course. Many students cover the monthly side but get caught by the launch side.

How proof of funds works

Most student visas require you to show that you can pay for tuition and living costs. The amount, the acceptable forms of evidence and the rules about timing are set by each government and updated over time, which is why this guide does not quote figures.

Common points to check on the official immigration website:

  • How much you must show. Some countries set a living-cost amount on top of first-year tuition; others rely on the costs your university lists, such as the estimated expenses on a US Form I-20.
  • What counts. Bank statements, education loans, scholarship letters and sponsor documents are often accepted, but each country has rules about whose accounts count and which lenders qualify. Canada, for example, accepts several forms of proof, including a Guaranteed Investment Certificate from a participating bank.
  • How long the money must be held. Some countries, including the UK, require funds to have been in your account for a set period before you apply.

Watch out: Never borrow money just to make a bank balance look bigger for a visa application. Misrepresenting your finances can lead to refusal and can affect future applications.

Remember that proof of funds is a minimum the government accepts, not a full budget. Your real costs may be higher, especially in expensive cities.

Build your budget in five steps

  1. Collect official numbers. Start with the university's published fees and estimated living costs, then the government's proof-of-funds requirement, and use whichever is higher as your floor.
  2. Price your actual life. Check current rent for the type of housing you will really live in, a monthly transit pass, and a grocery shop at a local supermarket.
  3. Add one-time costs. List deposits, visa fees, insurance, flights and setup purchases, with the month each is due.
  4. Add a buffer. Allow for exchange-rate movements, price rises in later years, and an emergency fund you leave untouched.
  5. Match money to months. Map savings, family support, loans and scholarships against each month's costs so you can see any gap before it happens.

Plan for currency risk

If your savings or family income are in one currency and your bills are in another, exchange rates can change what your course costs. Say your tuition is quoted in a currency that strengthens by 10% against yours between offer and payment. You would need roughly 10% more of your own currency to pay the same bill. That is a hypothetical, but swings of that size do happen.

  • Budget at a slightly worse exchange rate than today's.
  • Compare transfer providers on the total cost, including the exchange rate margin, not just the headline fee.
  • Consider converting large known payments, such as first-year tuition, once your plans are confirmed.
  • Understand which currency any education loan is in and how repayment works.

Do not count on part-time work for core costs

Many student visas allow some paid work, but the rules vary by country, visa type, level of study and time of year, and they change. Some countries cap weekly hours during term; others restrict off-campus work or allow work only in specific circumstances. In the US, F-1 students face tighter limits on off-campus work than in many other destinations.

Check the current rules on GOV.UK, IRCC, travel.state.gov or the Department of Home Affairs. Even where work is allowed, finding a job takes time. Treat earnings as a bonus for extras, not as the money that pays rent and tuition.

Ways to reduce what you spend

  • Apply widely for scholarships. Look at university awards, government schemes and organisations in your home country. Start with our scholarships page.
  • Consider lower-tuition systems. Public universities in Germany have long charged little or no tuition for many programmes, though students usually pay a semester contribution and some states and universities now charge non-EU students tuition. Check DAAD for current rules. Living costs, language of instruction and recognition at home still matter.
  • Look at course length. Many taught master's degrees in the UK take one year, and most bachelor's degrees in England take three. A shorter course can mean fewer months of living costs, but check that the qualification suits your goals.
  • Share housing and cook. Shared flats and self-catering residences usually cost less than studios and meal plans.
  • Use student discounts. Transit passes, software, museums and phone plans often have student rates.
  • Buy second-hand. Use the library and second-hand textbooks, and wait until you arrive to buy bulky household items.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need to study abroad?

It depends on the country, city, institution, programme length and lifestyle. Start with the university's published fees and living-cost estimate and the official proof-of-funds requirement, then build your own budget from real local prices.

Can an education loan count as proof of funds?

Often, yes, if it comes from an acceptable lender and meets the destination's rules. Each immigration authority defines what it accepts, so check the official guidance before you apply.

Can I pay my tuition from part-time work?

It is risky to plan that way. Work rights are limited and vary by country, jobs take time to find, and most visa authorities expect you to show funds before you arrive.

Is a low-tuition country always the cheapest option?

Not necessarily. Living costs, a longer programme, language courses or extra fees can outweigh lower tuition. Compare the full cost of the whole course.
