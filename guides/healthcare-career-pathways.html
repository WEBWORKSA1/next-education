Healthcare careers: pathways from certificate to doctorate

Map healthcare career ladders from certificate to doctorate in nursing, lab, imaging and health admin, and see how licensing differs by country and region.

Careers6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

Healthcare is one of the few fields where you can start with a short certificate and keep climbing, sometimes all the way to a doctorate, while you work. This guide maps the main ladders, explains how licensing changes the route depending on where you live, and shows you where to check pay and job outlook from official sources.

How healthcare career ladders work

Many healthcare credentials are stackable. Each rung is a job in its own right, and bridging programs at many colleges and universities recognise your earlier training so you don't start from zero. Working on one rung while studying for the next lets you earn, build experience and confirm the field suits you.

The catch is regulation. Most clinical titles are protected by law. To use one, you usually have to complete an approved program, pass a required exam, and register with the regulator where you work. Titles, entry routes and scope of practice differ by country, and in the US, Canada and Australia they can also differ by state, province or territory. Treat the ladders below as general patterns, and always confirm the details with your local regulator.

The nursing ladder

Nursing has the most clearly defined ladder in healthcare. The rungs below appear, under different names, in all five countries this guide covers.

RungTypical educationExample titles
Support workerShort certificate or employer trainingPersonal support worker or health care aide (Canada), certified nursing assistant (US), healthcare assistant (UK), assistant in nursing (Australia)
Practical or enrolled nurseDiploma or certificateLicensed practical or vocational nurse (US), registered or licensed practical nurse (Canada), enrolled nurse (Australia)
Registered nurseDegree in most places; associate degree is also an option in the USRegistered nurse; in India, programs such as B.Sc Nursing lead to registration
Advanced practiceGraduate degree plus additional certification or endorsementNurse practitioner and other advanced practice roles
DoctorateDoctoral degreeDoctor of Nursing Practice (clinical leadership) or PhD (research and teaching)

The ladder isn't identical everywhere. In England, the nursing associate is a regulated role that sits between healthcare assistant and registered nurse. In India, diploma routes such as ANM and GNM exist alongside degree programs. Support worker roles are regulated in some places and not in others; in the US, for example, nursing assistants complete state-approved training and appear on a state registry.

Each rung usually needs its own approved program, exam and registration. Earlier credentials may shorten the next step through a bridging program, but don't assume they will. Ask the program what credit it gives.

Who regulates nursing where you live

  • United States: each state's board of nursing issues licences. The National Council of State Boards of Nursing develops the NCLEX licensing exams.
  • Canada: provincial and territorial regulatory bodies, such as colleges of nurses, register nurses in their jurisdiction.
  • United Kingdom: the Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) registers nurses and midwives across the UK and nursing associates in England.
  • India: the Indian Nursing Council sets education standards, and state nursing councils register nurses.
  • Australia: the Nursing and Midwifery Board of Australia, supported by the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA), registers nurses and midwives.

Moving countries? Expect a credential assessment and possibly exams or extra coursework before you can register. Start with the destination regulator's official website rather than a recruitment agency.

Medical assistant and clinical support roles

In the US, medical assistants handle a mix of clinical and administrative work in clinics and physicians' offices. Training is usually a certificate or diploma, sometimes an associate degree. Several organisations offer certification, and many employers prefer or require it. Outside the US, similar work is split among roles such as medical office assistants and healthcare assistants, and the duties allowed vary.

These roles make good first rungs. They put you in a clinical setting quickly, and many people use them as a base for moving into nursing, health administration or a technologist program.

Laboratory and imaging technology

Medical laboratory

A common progression runs from laboratory assistant or phlebotomist (certificate) to laboratory technician (diploma or associate degree) to laboratory scientist or technologist (usually a bachelor's degree), then on to specialist, supervisory or master's-level roles. Title conventions differ: in the US, "technician" and "scientist" mark different education levels, while Canada uses "medical laboratory technologist" for its main professional credential. In the UK, biomedical scientists must register with the Health and Care Professions Council (HCPC).

Medical imaging

Imaging usually starts with an accredited program in radiography or radiologic technology, at diploma, associate or bachelor's level depending on the country. From there, many technologists add specialisations such as CT, MRI, mammography or ultrasound, and later move into lead, education or management roles. Radiographers are regulated by the HCPC in the UK and through AHPRA in Australia; in the US and Canada, licensing rules depend on the state or province. Some imaging specialties, such as ultrasound, are regulated differently, so check each one separately.

Health administration and public health

Not every healthcare career involves direct patient care. A typical administration ladder starts with a certificate in medical office administration or health records, moves to a diploma or degree in health information management or health administration, and can continue to a Master of Health Administration (MHA), Master of Public Health (MPH) or MBA. Doctoral options include the Doctor of Public Health (DrPH) and research PhDs. Professional associations in several countries offer certifications in health information management.

Public health has a similar shape: community health or health promotion roles at entry level, a bachelor's degree, then an MPH for epidemiology, policy or program leadership. Clinicians also cross over; a nurse might move into unit management and then complete an MHA.

Reaching the doctorate level

Clinical doctorates such as medicine, pharmacy and physiotherapy have their own entry routes. In the US and Canada, medicine usually follows an undergraduate degree. In the UK, India and parts of Australia, many students enter medicine straight from secondary school, and graduate-entry routes also exist. Doctors are licensed by state medical boards in the US, provincial colleges of physicians and surgeons in Canada, the General Medical Council in the UK, the National Medical Commission in India, and the Medical Board of Australia through AHPRA.

Check pay and outlook from official sources

Pay and demand vary by region, employer, union agreement and year, so check current figures rather than relying on numbers quoted in articles or advertisements. These official sources are updated regularly:

  • US: the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook, with duties, education, pay and outlook by occupation.
  • Canada: Job Bank, with wages and outlooks by occupation and region.
  • UK: the National Careers Service for England, and NHS Health Careers for NHS roles and training routes.
  • Australia: Your Career, the Australian Government's careers site.
  • India: the National Career Service portal run by the Ministry of Labour and Employment.

To choose your first rung, consider how much patient contact you want, whether shift work suits you, and how far up the ladder you might eventually go. Frequently asked questions

Can I keep working while I move up the ladder?

Often, yes. Many bridging and degree-completion programs offer part-time, evening or online coursework for working students. Clinical placements, though, may require daytime or block availability, so ask how placements are scheduled before you enrol.

Do I need a degree to become a registered nurse?

It depends on where you'll practise. In the US, both associate and bachelor's degrees can lead to RN licensure. Most of the other countries covered here require a degree, with some exceptions. Your regulator's website lists the approved programs.

Will my healthcare credential transfer if I move?

Not automatically, even between states or provinces in some cases. Regulators assess outside credentials individually and may require exams, supervised practice or extra coursework. Check with the regulator before you move.

Is a short certificate worth it if I plan to become a nurse?

It can be. A support worker or nursing assistant role gives you paid experience and confirms you like the work. Ask nursing programs whether they give admission preference or credit for that experience.
