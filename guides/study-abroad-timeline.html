Study abroad planning timeline: from 18 months out to arrival

A step-by-step study abroad timeline: when to shortlist, book IELTS or TOEFL, apply, chase scholarships, prove funds, get your visa and prepare to fly.

Study abroad6 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

Studying abroad rewards people who start early. This guide gives you a realistic sequence from about 18 months before your course starts to the week you land, what to do at each stage, and where to check the rules that change from year to year.

Start from your intake, not your flight

Every timeline works backwards from the date your course begins, and that date depends on the country. Most US universities admit mainly for a fall start, with smaller spring intakes. UK universities focus on September, with some January starts. Canadian institutions commonly start in September, with January and sometimes May intakes. Australia's academic year begins early in the calendar year, with a second intake mid-year. German universities run a winter semester starting in autumn and a summer semester starting in spring.

Application deadlines can fall close to a year before you start. US undergraduate early rounds and UK applications through UCAS for the most competitive courses close in the autumn of the year before entry. Scholarship deadlines are often earlier than admission deadlines.

Quick check: Write down your target intake and the earliest deadline on your shortlist. That deadline, not your departure date, is the real anchor for everything below.

The timeline, step by step

Treat the month ranges as a guide. Competitive programmes, scholarship applicants and anyone facing long visa processing should shift everything earlier.

  1. Research and shortlist (18 to 15 months out). Decide what you want from studying abroad: a specific programme, a career route, a language, or a post-study pathway. Use the official country portals (EducationUSA, EduCanada, Study UK, Study Australia and DAAD for Germany) to compare systems. Confirm that the qualification will be recognised by employers or regulators back home if you plan to return. Narrow your list to five to eight programmes.
  2. Plan your tests (15 to 12 months out). Most programmes taught in English ask non-native speakers for an English test such as IELTS, TOEFL iBT or PTE Academic, and some accept others; check each programme's list. Some US undergraduate programmes want the SAT or ACT, while many are test-optional, and some graduate programmes require the GRE or GMAT. Book early and leave room for a retake. For certain UK visa routes the English test must come from an approved list on GOV.UK.
  3. Gather documents (12 to 10 months out). Request official transcripts and certified translations, check that your passport will stay valid for your whole course, and ask referees for recommendation letters with plenty of notice. Draft your personal statement or statement of purpose, update your CV, and prepare a portfolio if your field needs one. If a university asks for a credential evaluation, use only the service it names.
  4. Submit applications (12 to 8 months out). US undergraduates usually apply through the Common App or a university portal. UK undergraduates apply through UCAS, which has an earlier deadline for Oxford, Cambridge and most medicine, dentistry and veterinary courses. Many German universities use uni-assist. Postgraduate applications elsewhere are often made directly to the university, sometimes on a rolling basis, so applying early can help.
  5. Apply for scholarships (in parallel, from 15 months out). Some awards are automatic with admission, others need a separate application months before entry. Government schemes such as Chevening, Fulbright, Australia Awards and DAAD scholarships have their own cycles. Track every deadline in one place.
  6. Compare offers and accept (8 to 5 months out). Note whether each offer is conditional or unconditional, what deposit is required and whether it is refundable. Accepting triggers the document your visa depends on: a Form I-20 or DS-2019 in the US, a Confirmation of Acceptance for Studies (CAS) in the UK, a letter of acceptance in Canada, or a Confirmation of Enrolment (CoE) in Australia.
  7. Organise finances and proof of funds (8 to 4 months out). Most student visas require evidence that you can pay tuition and living costs. Countries set their own rules on how much, what kind of funds count and how long the money must have been held. Line up savings, loans or sponsor letters now so the paperwork is ready when you apply.
  8. Apply for your visa or study permit (4 to 2 months out, earlier where processing is slow). Expect some combination of online forms, fees, biometrics, an interview and possibly a medical exam. Processing times vary by country, season and where you apply from, so check the official estimate before you plan anything else. Do not book non-refundable flights until your visa is approved.
  9. Arrange accommodation (4 to 2 months out). University housing applications often open once you accept an offer and can fill quickly. If you rent privately, verify the listing and landlord before paying anything, and never send money for a place nobody can show you.
  10. Sort out health insurance (3 to 1 months out). Requirements differ sharply. Australia requires Overseas Student Health Cover for student visa holders; UK visa applicants usually pay the Immigration Health Surcharge; many US universities require you to join their student plan or prove equivalent cover; Canadian coverage depends on the province.
  11. Prepare to leave (2 months to 1 week out). Book flights, gather medical records, pack prescriptions with documentation, and keep your passport, visa, acceptance letter and proof of funds in your hand luggage. Register for orientation and plan how you will get from the airport to your accommodation.

Where to check the rules that change

Visa conditions, fees, financial thresholds and work rights change regularly, so this guide deliberately does not quote them. Go to the official source for your destination:

  • United States: student visa information on travel.state.gov, with free advising through EducationUSA centres.
  • Canada: study permit rules on canada.ca (IRCC).
  • United Kingdom: the Student visa pages on GOV.UK.
  • Australia: the Student visa pages from the Department of Home Affairs.
  • Germany: DAAD explains entry requirements and links to the German missions that issue visas.

Your university's international student office is the next best source, because it deals with these rules every intake.

Common mistakes that cost time

  • Booking an English test too close to the deadline, leaving no room for a retake.
  • Missing a scholarship because its deadline came before the admission deadline.
  • Assuming proof-of-funds rules are the same as a friend's who went to a different country or applied in a different year.
  • Paying for flights or private housing before the visa is approved.

If you are starting late

Less than a year out is not automatically too late, but you need to prioritise. Look for programmes with later or rolling deadlines and for intakes later in the year, such as January starts in the UK and Canada or mid-year starts in Australia. Book your English test immediately. Check visa processing times before you commit, because a late offer is no use if the visa cannot arrive before the course starts.

If the dates do not work, deferring to the next intake is often smarter than rushing an application that undersells you.

Frequently asked questions

How early should I start planning to study abroad?

Around 12 to 18 months before your course begins is a comfortable window for most people. Start closer to 18 months if you need scholarships, plan to apply to highly competitive programmes, or expect a long visa process.

Can I apply for a student visa before I have an offer?

Generally no. Student visas usually depend on a document issued by the institution after you accept an offer, such as an I-20 in the US, a CAS in the UK, a letter of acceptance in Canada or a CoE in Australia. Check the official immigration site for your destination.

How long are English test scores valid?

Many institutions accept IELTS and TOEFL results for about two years, but each university and visa authority sets its own rule. Check that your score will still be valid on the date your application or visa is assessed.

Do I need an education agent?

No. Many students apply directly using official portals and university websites. If you use an agent, ask how they are paid, and verify key information about visas and fees on official government sites.
