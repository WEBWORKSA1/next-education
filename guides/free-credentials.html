Free and Low-Cost Ways to Earn Credentials and College Credit

How to earn credentials and college credit for little or nothing: MOOCs, open courseware, credit by exam, prior learning, employer aid and apprenticeships.

Learning5 min readUpdated October 30, 2026By the Next.Education Editorial Team · How we research

You don't always need to pay full tuition to learn new skills or earn credit toward a qualification. This guide covers the main free and low-cost routes, from open courses and credit by exam to employer funding and paid apprenticeships, and explains how to make those credentials count with employers and colleges.

Free courses: MOOCs and open courseware

MOOCs (massive open online courses) are offered by universities and companies through large online platforms. Many let you audit course materials free and charge only for a verified certificate, graded assignments or credit. Some platforms offer financial aid for certificates. Audit and pricing policies change, so check the course page before you enrol.

Open courseware goes further. MIT OpenCourseWare publishes materials from a large number of MIT courses, including lecture notes, problem sets and exams, free and without registration. It doesn't award credit or certificates, but it's excellent preparation. OpenStax, based at Rice University, publishes free, peer-reviewed, openly licensed textbooks for many introductory college courses.

In India, the government's SWAYAM platform offers free online courses from Indian universities and institutions, with an optional proctored exam for a fee if you want a certificate. Under UGC regulations, universities can accept SWAYAM credits toward their own programs. NPTEL, run by the IITs and IISc, offers free engineering and science courses with optional paid certification exams.

Quick check: A smart low-cost combination is free open courseware for learning, then a credit-by-exam test or a credit-bearing course to prove it.

Earn college credit by exam

If you already know a subject, you may be able to test out of a course instead of paying to take it. In the US, the College Board's CLEP program offers exams in introductory college subjects such as composition, mathematics, sciences, business and languages. Many colleges grant credit for passing scores, but each sets its own policy on which exams it accepts, the minimum score and how many credits it will award.

Other routes include DSST exams and, for high-school students, Advanced Placement and International Baccalaureate results. Outside the US, ask whether your college offers challenge exams, which let you earn credit for a course by passing its final assessment.

Before you book any exam, get your target institution's credit policy in writing. A passing score is only valuable if the college you'll attend accepts it.

Get credit for what you already know

Work, military service, volunteering and professional training can all count toward a qualification. The process goes by different names:

  • US: prior learning assessment (PLA), often through a portfolio or a review of workplace training.
  • Canada: prior learning assessment and recognition (PLAR), offered by many colleges and universities.
  • UK: recognition or accreditation of prior learning (RPL or APL).
  • Australia: recognition of prior learning (RPL), widely used in vocational education.
  • India: the RPL component of the government's Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY) skills program certifies existing skills.

Institutions usually cap how much of a qualification you can earn this way. Ask early, keep evidence of your experience, and expect to document what you learned, not just what you did.

Let someone else pay: employer and government programs

Employer tuition assistance is one of the most underused benefits. Ask HR whether your employer pays for courses, certifications or degrees, and read the terms: many require good grades, job relevance or staying with the company for a period afterward.

Government-funded training exists in every country this guide covers, though programs change often. A few established examples:

  • US: American Job Centers can connect eligible job-seekers to training funded under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act. CareerOneStop, from the US Department of Labor, helps you find a centre.
  • UK: in England, Skills Bootcamps and the Free Courses for Jobs offer give eligible adults free training. Check eligibility on GOV.UK.
  • Canada: provinces run retraining programs, such as Better Jobs Ontario. Check your provincial government's employment pages.
  • Australia: Fee-Free TAFE places cover tuition for eligible students in selected courses. Check your state or territory's TAFE site.
  • India: SWAYAM, NPTEL and PMKVY, described above.

Community college and college pathways

Starting at a lower-cost institution and transferring can cut the cost of a degree considerably. In the US, many community colleges have transfer agreements with four-year universities, and some states run free or reduced-tuition community college programs. In Canada, provincial transfer guides, such as ONTransfer in Ontario and the BC Transfer Guide, show which college courses count at which universities.

In the UK, the Access to Higher Education Diploma prepares adults without traditional qualifications for university. In Australia, many TAFE qualifications give credit toward related university degrees. In every case, confirm transfer credit with the receiving institution before you enrol.

Apprenticeships: earn while you learn

Apprenticeships combine paid work with structured training and lead to a recognised qualification, usually without tuition debt. In the US, Registered Apprenticeships are listed on apprenticeship.gov. In the UK, you can search vacancies on GOV.UK, and apprenticeships range from entry level to degree level. In Canada, apprenticeships are run provincially, and the interprovincial Red Seal Program sets a common standard for many trades. Australia and India also run national apprenticeship schemes.

Apprenticeships are no longer limited to traditional trades. Depending on the country, you'll find them in IT, healthcare support, business and engineering.

How to make free credentials count with employers

A certificate alone rarely gets you hired. What employers want is evidence you can do the work. Strengthen your signal:

  • Build one or two portfolio projects that apply what you learned, and link to them.
  • List credentials in a dedicated section with the issuer, date and a verification link where available.
  • Stack related credentials into a clear story, such as three data courses plus a project, rather than listing unrelated certificates.
  • Be accurate: an audited course is not a certificate, and a certificate is not college credit.
  • Prepare to discuss what you built and learned in interviews; skills tests are common.

Frequently asked questions

Are free online certificates worth putting on a résumé?

Yes, if they're relevant to the job and you can show what you learned. They carry the most weight alongside projects or work experience that use the skills.

Will my college accept MOOC or CLEP credit?

It depends entirely on the institution. Check its transfer and credit-by-exam policies, and get confirmation in writing before you spend money on exams or certificates.

Can adults do apprenticeships?

In many countries, yes. Age rules vary by country and program, so check the official apprenticeship site where you live.
