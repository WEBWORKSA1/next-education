How the calculator works

Your total investment is the program cost minus scholarships and grants, plus the income you give up while studying. That last part is the one most people skip, and for full-time programs it's often bigger than tuition.

Each year after you finish, you gain the difference between your expected salary and your current salary. We grow that gap by the rate you choose and add it up. The payback period is the point where the running total turns positive. The 10-year return is the net gain divided by what you put in.

Getting the inputs right

  • Expected salary: use official sources, such as the US Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook, Canada's Job Bank or the UK National Careers Service, and the school's own graduate-outcome reports. Use the median, not the top figure in a brochure.
  • Current salary: what you would realistically earn without the program over the same years.
  • Loans: interest raises the real cost. Run the loan calculator and add total interest to the program cost for a stricter test.
  • Taxes: this is a pre-tax comparison. That's fine for comparing options against each other.

Rule of thumb: payback within about five years is a strong financial case. Beyond ten years, look hard at cheaper routes, employer sponsorship or part-time study.

Money isn't the only return. Some careers are worth it for the work itself. The point is to choose knowingly. Read Is a master's or MBA worth it? for the full framework.

ROI calculator questions

Why is lost income included?

Because it's a real cost. If you leave a job to study full-time, the salary you don't earn is part of what the program costs you. Part-time and online programs often win on ROI for exactly this reason.

What growth rate should I use?

Something modest, like 1 to 3 percent, is a sensible default. It reflects the pay gap widening slightly as you progress. Use 0 for a conservative test.

Is my data saved?

No. Everything is calculated in your browser and nothing is sent to us.
